# Fix the seams before swapping the encoder

The span head is already the right architecture. The next gains for our multilingual PII model will most likely come from four places, all cheap at inference. The first is how predictions are stitched across window overlaps and how span decisions are thresholded. The second is span-aware regularisation, mainly boundary smoothing. The third is data that forces the model to read context: hard look-alike negatives, entity swaps and code-switch consistency. The fourth is CPU engineering, specifically ONNX int8 and a smaller window overlap. Our own results fit the literature closely. GlobalPointer (SPAN) gained +0.013 on ml_struct_dev and +0.017 on ml_wm_dev over the v5.0 softmax head, with the largest gain on ADDRESS. That matches published gains of +0.05 to +3.1 F1 for GlobalPointer over BERT-CRF, and the general pattern that span heads help long entities. Head-to-head comparisons put every modern flat-NER head (biaffine, W2NER, PIQN, DiffusionNER, semi-CRF) within a few tenths of a point of each other, so swapping SPAN for another head is a poor bet. The encoder literature argues against expecting accuracy from our in-flight swaps. mmBERT and EuroBERT both trail XLM-R on NER. A causal decoder such as our Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B run loses 15-17 F1 against the same model with its attention unmasked. Generative and universal NER (GLiNER, UniNER, GNER) roughly ties a fine-tuned encoder at best when in-domain supervision exists, and costs far more. The biggest methodological risk is that our gains are single-seed. Published seed noise for NER is about 1 F1 point. Our next experiment should therefore be a seed run that tells us which of the 0.005-0.013 deltas are real.

## Span heads have converged; the head itself is no longer the bottleneck

Across 2020-2023, every span or structured head lands in the same narrow band on flat English benchmarks: CoNLL03 at roughly 92.8-94.0 and OntoNotes 5 at 90.5-91.9. Within any single paper, the gain over a strong tagger or biaffine baseline is usually +0.1 to +0.7 F1. GlobalPointer scores each typed span as a RoPE-rotated query-key product. It is a low-rank, position-aware cousin of the biaffine scorer. Against BERT-CRF it reported **+0.74 on CLUENER, +1.59 on CMeEE and +3.11 on CoNLL04**, but only +0.05 on People's Daily. Removing RoPE cost 11 points ([Su et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.03054)). Our +0.013 on struct and +0.017 on wm sit inside that range, and the ADDRESS (+0.058) and BUSINESS gains match the literature's finding that span heads beat taggers on long entities while taggers win on short ones ([SpanNER, Fu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00641)). No paper compares GlobalPointer directly with biaffine or W2NER on the same encoder. W2NER's flat results (CoNLL03 93.07, OntoNotes 90.50) fall below biaffine with boundary smoothing (93.65 / 91.74) ([W2NER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10070); [Boundary Smoothing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.12031)). PIQN, DiffusionNER, CNN-Nested-NER and Locate-and-Label were built for nested NER, and their flat gains are within noise ([PIQN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10545); [DiffusionNER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13298); [Yan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.04534)). We have 8 flat types, so none of these deserves a training run.

The useful levers are the ones that do not change how spans are scored. **Boundary smoothing** moves a fraction ε of each gold span's probability onto spans whose boundaries lie within distance D of the gold boundary. It improved all 8 flat and nested English and Chinese benchmarks tested: **CoNLL03 +0.17, OntoNotes 5 +0.53, MSRA +0.46, OntoNotes 4 +0.80, Weibo +1.25, Resume +0.32**. It mostly raised precision, the gain held across BERT, RoBERTa and MacBERT at base and large sizes, and calibration improved ([Zhu & Li, ACL 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.12031)). Plain label smoothing over types did not help and sometimes hurt (ACE05 86.56 → 85.65). The benefit is specific to boundaries, which is exactly where strict span F1 loses points on PERSON titles, trailing ADDRESS postcodes and "Sdn Bhd"-style BUSINESS suffixes. One caveat: our GlobalPointer loss is a multi-label categorical cross-entropy, and no published soft-label version exists. The paper's own ablation shows plain BCE costs only 0.09 on CoNLL04 ([Su et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.03054)), so switching to BCE first is low-risk. **Efficient GlobalPointer** factorises the scorer into a shared "is this an entity" term plus a cheap per-type classifier. It gained +0.60 on CLUENER and +0.56 on CMeEE but lost 0.15 on People's Daily ([Su et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.03054)). With only 8 types, expect little. **Span-length features** added +0.65 on CoNLL03 in SpanNER, and heuristic non-overlap decoding added +0.32 ([Fu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00641)). We already decode greedily without overlap, so that gain is banked, and RoPE already encodes span width, so a learned width prior is a weak bet. **Deep span representations (DSpERT)** build span vectors with a span-level Transformer instead of pooling the top layer. They beat shallow pooling by more than 30 F1 on spans longer than 10 tokens on ACE04, and reached CoNLL03 93.64±0.06 against 93.21 for shallow pooling. Training costs about 5×, and two span layers recover most of the gain ([Zhu & Li 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04182)). Our AL8 result (+0.008 struct at the token level) suggests depth helps a little. DSpERT-lite would test whether depth at the span level helps more on ADDRESS. **Filtered semi-Markov CRF** beat token CRF by +1.14 on CoNLL03 and +1.43 on OntoNotes with bert-large, and decodes in 0.2 ms per sample against 2.7-4.4 ms for CRF ([Zaratiana et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18028)). It is the only structured-decoding alternative with a credible flat-NER gain over CRF, but it would replace SPAN rather than add to it.

Cost is not a constraint for the cheap heads. Our back-of-envelope estimate puts the GlobalPointer projections plus pairwise scores at under 1% of XLM-R base's roughly 66 GFLOPs per 384-token window, which agrees with our measured 24.8 s vs 25.6 s CPU. The expensive heads are W2NER's dilated 2D convolutions over 147k grid cells, PL-Marker (54.8 vs 138.7 sentences/s against a tagger on an A100, [Ye et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.06067)), DSpERT, and anything generative or diffusion-based.

The window seam is a gap the literature has not studied, but it follows from what we know. Our predictor resolves the 96-token overlap per token by keeping the most confident window. Cross-sentence work with BERT found that tokens near a window edge get worse predictions, and that taking each token's label from several windows (Contextual Majority Voting) matched or beat single-window labelling: **Finnish 93.39 → 93.66, German 85.21 → 85.63, Spanish 87.06 → 87.89** ([Luoma & Pyysalo, COLING 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.coling-main.78.pdf)). For a span head, the natural version is to average the (start, end, type) scores across both windows before greedy decoding. Long ADDRESS spans cut at a window edge are the case most likely to benefit.

## Modern encoders buy speed, not accuracy, and causal decoders need unmasking

Nothing published suggests that mmBERT, EuroBERT or a decoder LLM will beat XLM-R base on token classification. On WikiANN (cross-lingual zero-shot), **mmBERT-base scores 58.2 and mmBERT-small 54.3, against 61.4 for XLM-R base**. mmBERT still wins XNLI and TyDiQA. Its authors attribute the NER deficit to inconsistent prefix-whitespace tokens in pre-training ([mmBERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06888)). Our mmBERT-small validation macro-F1 of .938 against .945 is a much smaller gap than WikiANN predicts. In-domain supervision narrows the gap, as expected. The real question for mmBERT-small is therefore latency per F1 point, and its reported "about 2× faster than base" is a GPU figure that has to be measured on CPU. EuroBERT says itself that it "lags behind XLM-RoBERTa" on NER, and it was pre-trained on only 6 of our 12 languages. **Korean, Thai, Malay, Indonesian and Tagalog are missing** ([EuroBERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05500)). Report its F1 split by covered and uncovered languages, and do not expect it to win. The literature's accuracy candidates are elsewhere. RemBERT beats XLM-R on XTREME NER (70.1 vs 65.4) ([Chung et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12821)), XLM-R large beats base on English WikiAnn (84.0 vs 82.7) ([T-NER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.12616)), and mDeBERTa-v3 was "very similar or better" than XLM-R on Slovak NER ([WikiGoldSK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.04026)). All three are slower on CPU, so their realistic role is as teachers.

The Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B run needs a warning attached. With the causal mask kept during fine-tuning, **Llama2-7B scores 0.744 test F1 on CoNLL03; unmasking all layers gives 0.908, and unmasking the best layer groups gives 0.920**. Mistral-7B shows the same pattern (0.756 → 0.927). Unmasking only the upper layers is usually best, but **at 68M parameters unmasking gave no gain and encoders matched or beat small decoders** ([Dukić & Šnajder, Findings ACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14556v3)). LLM2Vec adds a second warning. Removing the mask without adaptation cut frozen-probe NER for LLaMA-family models by about 4 points (S-LLaMA-1.3B 96.09 → 92.17), while a short masked-next-token-prediction (MNTP) phase recovered the loss and added a gain (96.59). The sentence-level contrastive stage, which is how embedding models are trained, slightly hurt token-level probes ([LLM2Vec](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05961)). Our causal Qwen3 run is therefore expected to trail XLM-R. Its first token of "Lee" cannot see whether "Road" or "Hsien Loong" follows, which hits exactly the ADDRESS and BUSINESS types whose type is decided by later tokens. The informative follow-up changes one variable: the attention mask, from causal to bidirectional, on the same checkpoint. At about 2× XLM-R base's parameters, Qwen3 is a teacher candidate, not a deployment candidate.

Generative and universal NER is a poor fit for supervised PII. **In-domain supervised, GLiNER-L (0.3B) averages 82.9 F1 against 84.8 for UniNER-7B across 20 datasets.** On multilingual zero-shot MultiCoNER, GLiNER-Multi scores 32.9 against 54.9 for a supervised XLM-R ([GLiNER](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08526)). PII-specialised GLiNER variants reach only **0.37-0.47 exact-span F1 on SPY**, and the newest, GLiNER2-PII, was trained on 7 European languages ([GLiNER2-PII](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09973)). OpenAI's 1.5B Privacy Filter is overtaken by a fine-tuned XLM-R after about 100-1,000 labelled examples, and it **collapses to 0.04 F1 on Arabic** ([OPF evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02616)). Autoregressive generation over 36.8k-token documents would also be orders of magnitude slower than our 25 s CPU budget. The one useful thing to take from this line of work is LLM- or teacher-labelled pre-training data. NuNER showed that 1M GPT-3.5-annotated sentences lift a RoBERTa-base encoder by 10-15 F1 in few-shot settings, but the gain shrinks as labelled data grows ([NuNER](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15343)). In GLiNER, the same kind of pre-training added only +0.8 when supervised data was available ([GLiNER](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.08526)). With our data volume, expect 0-1 F1, and most of it on real rather than synthetic documents.

## Context, code-switching and look-alikes are data problems more than model problems

Document context matters, but we already have most of it. FLERT added 64 subtokens of surrounding context to sentence-level XLM-R large and gained **+1.15 F1 on average**. Enforcing document boundaries, so that no context leaks in from a neighbouring document, raised scores further (EN 93.64 → 93.75, NL 94.87 → 95.21). Context sizes from 48 to 128 subtokens performed almost identically ([FLERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06993)). Our 384-token windows already exceed that context size, so the transferable lessons are the boundary rule and the window-seam policy, not longer windows. Boundary enforcement applies when one file holds several logical documents: email threads, concatenated KYC forms, stacked statements. Retrieval-augmented NER won MultiCoNER 2022 and 2023 ([DAMO-NLP 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00545); [U-RaNER 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03688)), but client PII cannot go to a search engine, and account numbers are not in Wikipedia. Cooperative learning is the part worth taking. During training, a KL loss pulls the plain-input view toward a context-rich view, and only the plain view is served. That kept **+1.5 to +2.1 F1 on WNUT with no retrieval at inference** ([Wang et al., ACL 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03654)). For us, the rich view could be a prepended document-type or section header.

For code-switching, consistency training is the best-supported multilingual method. ConNER enforces KL agreement between an entity span and its translation on unlabelled target text, plus dropout consistency on labelled data. It gained **+2.61 F1 on average in zero-shot transfer, with Arabic up 6.6 and Chinese up 6.1**. The dropout-consistency component alone, essentially R-Drop on labelled data, gave +1.3 ([ConNER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.09394)). Two cautions apply. Our setting is supervised in every language, so the zero-shot gains are an upper bound. And consistency on *translated labelled* data scored below vanilla (77.23 vs 77.68), which suggests label-projection noise in translate-train hurts. The closest analogue to our code-switch slice is a MultiCoNER code-mixed system. Mention-replacement augmentation raised dev macro-F1 from 75.93 to 77.19, PGD adversarial training alone did nothing for code-mixed data (75.96), but augmentation and PGD together reached **78.21 (+2.28)**. PGD did help Chinese, by +1.06 ([Qtrade AI, SemEval-2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07459)). Adversarial training is therefore a language-specific tool, not a code-switching one. The MultiCoNER II organisers also found that typo-noised test data cost systems about 10% F1 on average ([MultiCoNER II overview](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06586)). Our ml_real slice probably contains OCR- and typing-style noise of this kind.

Entity-context bias is where the most directly transferable recent work sits. Ma et al. measured how much information the entity and the context each carry, using pointwise V-information (PVI). In all six datasets they studied, the entity carried more information than the context. In other words, models learn surface forms. Replacing 40% of redundant easy entities with hard ones (Redundant2Low) left the dataset the same size and moved BERT on CoNLL03 from **91.18 / 70.89 / 44.48 to 90.42 / 77.34 / 48.07** (in-distribution / OOV / cross-category F1). The context-side variant lifted cross-category F1 by **+11.2** at a cost of 1.46 in-distribution, and the fixes stacked with MINER's mutual-information loss ([Ma et al., EMNLP 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.281.pdf)). In PII terms, the "hard entities" are account numbers that look like amounts or postcodes, and Korean account numbers formatted like resident or tax numbers. Only a cue word such as "계좌번호", "A/C No." or "주민등록번호" can tell these apart. The mirror case is account-shaped amounts and postcodes labelled O. FiNER reached the same conclusion for financial numerics. The label of a numeric token "depends mostly on context, not the token itself". Subword-fragmented numbers hurt BERT, and shape pseudo-tokens such as [XX.X] fixed much of the damage ([FiNER, ACL 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06482v2)). Number-shape normalisation is riskier for us, because XLM-R already sees our digits and native digit systems vary by script. Hard negatives cost nothing at inference and target our known Korean ACCOUNT/TIN confusion head-on. All of this evidence comes from PER/ORG/LOC or XBRL tags, so how well it transfers to PII identifiers is untested.

## PII systems win on thresholds, validators and evaluation, not exotic heads

PII benchmarks reward generalisation more than architecture. Detectors trained on synthetic data transfer poorly. **NVIDIA GLiNER-PII scores 0.87 strict F1 on its own Nemotron data, 0.70 on Argilla and 0.64 on AI4Privacy** ([nvidia/gliner-PII](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/gliner-PII)). On PIIBench, a merger of 10 sources, every off-the-shelf system scored below 0.14 span F1 ([PIIBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15776)). A plain DeBERTa fine-tune with weighted cross-entropy reached 0.65 there and beat both hierarchical and curriculum variants. Its authors credit diverse data and the loss weighting, not architecture ([arXiv 2605.25816](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.25816)). A robustness study of spaCy, Presidio and Qwen2.5-3B under distribution shift found that **"no single architecture is reliable across all PII categories"**. The three fail differently: boundary errors for NER, non-standard formats for rules, type confusion for LLMs ([Mind the Gap](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03464)). This supports what we already do with per-source and per-format slices (Kiii, ml_real, known-account-format). It also supports reporting leak-rate-style metrics alongside strict F1, following the Text Anonymization Benchmark (TAB), which counts an entity as protected only if all of its mentions are masked ([TAB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00443v2)).

The tricks that recur among winning PII systems are cheap. TAB shows **class weighting as an explicit recall dial**. Weighting the mask class 5× or 10× against O moved Longformer recall from 0.860 to 0.916 or 0.935, while precision fell from 0.925 to 0.843 or 0.795 ([TAB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00443v2)). According to a third-party write-up, the top Kaggle PII solutions combined O-class loss weights as low as 0.05, per-label decision thresholds tuned with Optuna, long windows, ensembles of diverse heads and rule-based post-filters ([Zenn retrospective](https://zenn.dev/sinchir0/articles/396967387196dc); [1st-place repo](https://github.com/ympaik87/pii-detection-1st-place)). The competition scored F5, though, so those settings over-favour recall for our strict-F1 target. With GlobalPointer, the equivalent knob is a per-label span-score threshold (default 0), tuned on dev. In industry, regex, checksum and context validators sit next to the model. Presidio's IBAN recognizer accepts a match only if it passes mod-97 and a per-country length check, and otherwise drops it ([Presidio IBAN recognizer](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/presidio/main/presidio-analyzer/presidio_analyzer/predefined_recognizers/generic/iban_recognizer.py)). Under our rule that account formats must be verified, a validator can only encode published checksums: IBAN mod-97, the ABA routing checksum, and documented national TIN check digits. Its job is to remove or relabel model spans, never to invent them, so it can only raise precision. No published ablation measures the F1 effect of adding validators to a neural detector, so we would be the first to measure it.

## Data-efficient training is noisy at our scale; distillation and int8 are where latency lives

The regularisers in the brief all report gains of about +0.2 to +1.0 F1, which is the size of seed noise. On MultiCoNER, R-Drop gave **+0.22 macro-F1** on the multilingual track, FGM gave +1.03 and PGD +0.51, and all of them were neutral or negative on code-mixed data, in single runs ([Qtrade AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07459)). Reimers & Gurevych found that changing only the seed moved NER F1 by about 1 point, with significant differences between seeds ([EMNLP 2017](https://aclanthology.org/D17-1035/)). Dodge et al. found that head initialisation and data order contribute variance in roughly equal parts ([Dodge et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06305)). A standard power calculation gives n ≈ 15.7σ²/Δ² seeds per arm. If our σ is 0.003, confirming a +0.005 gain takes about 6 seeds per arm; at σ = 0.005 it takes about 16. Paired seeds and a document-level bootstrap reduce that number. Our AL2/AL4 results, already judged "within noise", are an example of exactly this problem. The best-supported zero-cost training change is simply **training longer**. Two ICLR 2021 studies found that common BERT fine-tuning recipes stop too early. Once training length and Adam bias correction are fixed, tricks such as layer-wise LR decay and Mixout add little ([Mosbach et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04884); [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05987)). Three epochs is on the short side for a span head initialised from scratch. Checkpoint averaging and model soups add variance reduction at no inference cost ([Model soups](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05482)).

Distillation is the route to a faster model that keeps F1. XtremeDistil compressed multilingual mBERT NER across 41 languages. **A 28M-parameter student kept 88.64 average F1 against 91.86**, and the most aggressive setting reached up to 51× lower batch latency. The amount of unlabelled transfer data mattered ([XtremeDistil, ACL 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.202)). No paper distils a GlobalPointer span matrix specifically, so treating KL on temperature-softened span logits as the target is our own extrapolation. The natural pipeline is a slow teacher, then pseudo-labelled real wealth-management and code-switched text, then a fast student. The teacher could be XLM-R large or a bidirectional Qwen3; the student could be mmBERT-small or a 6-layer XLM-R that keeps our tokenizer and window code unchanged.

On CPU, engineering beats architecture. ONNX Runtime dynamic int8 combined with 25% structured pruning cut BioBERT NER latency from **32.52 ms to 12.02 ms (2.6×) while F1 moved from 0.954 to 0.948** ([Chalmers thesis](https://odr.chalmers.se/items/46a4159a-1ee3-4960-b0e3-1c13cfa17a03/full)). Static int8 on DistilBERT lost 0.26 accuracy points at about 3× lower latency ([Optimum static quantisation](https://www.philschmid.de/static-quantization-optimum)). Neither is an int8-only XLM-R NER result, so the F1 cost has to be measured on our slices. Our own window arithmetic matters as much: 129 windows × 384 tokens process 49.5k tokens to label 36.8k, so **about 35% of encoder compute goes to the overlap**. Cutting the overlap from 96 to 64 removes roughly a tenth of the windows. Vocabulary trimming keeps performance with about half of a multilingual vocabulary and cuts memory sharply, but barely changes compute, since the embedding lookup is cheap ([Ushio et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15020)). Token-level early exit (TOKEE) reports 66-75% lower inference cost on NER with minimal loss ([Li et al., ACL 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13878)), but our notes did not re-verify those numbers, and the method is research-grade. Try it only after int8 and distillation.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what we should be asking. The question is no longer which head or encoder is best, because published head comparisons are converged and modern multilingual encoders have not beaten XLM-R on NER. The question is where strict span F1 actually leaks: at window seams, at fuzzy boundaries, on numerics that only context can disambiguate, and on English+X text. Each of those leaks has a training-time or inference-time fix that costs nothing at CPU inference, which matters more than any head swap, given our 25 s per document budget. The one surprise in the evidence is how strongly the decoder-LLM papers predict that our causal Qwen3 run will fail. A disappointing number from that run should be read as an attention-mask problem, not as evidence about decoder LLMs.

Two things remain unknown, and both bound how confident we can be. No published study evaluates any of these methods on PII identifiers or on Thai, Tagalog, Malay or Indonesian, so every expected gain above is borrowed from PER/ORG/LOC benchmarks. And our own gains so far are single-seed and the same size as published seed noise. The seed run at rank 0 is cheap compared with the queue it gates. Running it first decides whether the queue is chasing real gains or noise.
